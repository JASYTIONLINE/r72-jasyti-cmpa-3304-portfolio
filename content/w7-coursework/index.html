Assignment 02: Your Own Web Inspiration

Discussion 03: The Living Web

1. Revisit Laurel Schwulst's essay "My website is a shifting house...". Now that you have a live Course Portfolio (and Course Homepage) and a Personal Landing Page that you have been building and updating for weeks, how does that change your perception of the essay? What resonates differently now that you have experience?

I first read Schwulst’s essay in Week 01. I understood the metaphors—a website as a house, a room, a garden, a structure you shape. However, I felt like the metaphor didn’t fully fit the concept of a website. Now that I’ve actually built and lived inside my own website for weeks, the metaphor hits differently.

I actually started creating the website as a personal project. I did not even know at the time that creating a portfolio was going to be the running theme of the class; it just seemed like a way to organize and feature my work. When I started, my site was the equivalent of four walls and a roof. I just needed a container to hold my work with links that operated correctly so navigation was possible. The goal was to be able to store and retrieve content. It was basic, functional, but empty—nothing more than an interactive box.

After the first week, I started to see ways to improve the UI/UX of the site, show off my skills as a front-end developer, and improve my coding skills. The site began to take on personality. Over time, as I applied the CSS, layout, interactivity, and navigation, the container became an environment. Things that were acceptable compromises in the first week became "must change" as the site progressed. It changed shape while maintaining the same functionality. The structure grew more stable; the layout became more thoughtful; the design choices became intentional instead of accidental. My website today looks nothing like the one I first imagined. It evolved as my skills evolved, which is the part that resonates now: a website really is a living, breathing thing.

It shifts as you grow. It improves as you improve. Every time I log in, I see something new I want to refine—spacing, typography, layout, a bit of color, or even rethinking the structure altogether. That cycle of build, evaluate, improve is exactly what Schwulst meant when she described websites as constantly changing spaces that reflect the person shaping them. But the biggest shift for me is the sense of ownership. Because I hand-coded every page, section, and line of CSS, the site feels like mine. I know how it works. I know where the walls stand and what's behind the sheetrock. I know how the plumbing runs and connects, why it works, and what breaks it. If I had built this with a drag-and-drop WYSIWYG tool, I wouldn’t feel connected to it the same way. Building it by hand made the site personal—not just something I made, but something I understand.
